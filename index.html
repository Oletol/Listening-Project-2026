English Pronunciation

The American Way

Designed for non-native speakers, the audiobook teaches American English pronunciation through phonemes, their allophones, and the systematic sound changes of connected speech. It combines explanation with practice for the tongue, lips, and jaw, using sound isolation, words, sentences, and minimal pairs.

Theory and practice · Week 2

A little theory before practice

Use the model below to understand how syllables are built and why spelling cannot reliably show English vowel sounds.

Syllable theory

The nucleus is the only indispensable part.

A syllable organises speech around a sonorous centre. The onset and coda are optional consonant positions; the nucleus is normally a vowel, although a syllabic consonant can sometimes fill that role.

Optional positions
A syllable may have no onset or no coda, but it must have a nucleus.
Boundary case: button
The recording names button as an example of a syllabic consonant. It does not ask learners to analyse its second syllable here.

Theory and practice · Week 3

How to read the vowel diagram

The diagram is an abstract map of tongue position. Read its two axes first; then add lip rounding and muscular tension as extra information.

Vowel-map theory

Two coordinates locate a vowel.

Tongue height runs vertically from high to low. Advancement runs horizontally from front to back. The diagram does not show the literal shape of the mouth; it gives learners a consistent way to compare vowel qualities.

Height
How high or low the tongue is positioned: high, mid, or low.
Advancement
Whether the tongue is positioned toward the front, centre, or back of the mouth.
Extra features
High and mid-back vowels are rounded in the model; tense and lax vowels differ in muscular effort and location.
Practice 01

Locate the vowel

For each sound, choose both coordinates on the simplified diagram. The words give you an auditory anchor, not the answer.

Practice 02

Follow the diphthong
